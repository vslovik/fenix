Updraft is a consumer lender: smaller loans used to repay more expensive debt, ~50,000 loans. The platform is a set of microservices — at one end customers register and apply, at the other they repay.

I was the principal contributor to the engine at the repayment end. The engine exists for the abnormal paths, each of which has to stay legislation-compliant — missed and late payments, forbearance, repayment-day changes, write-offs, early settlement ahead of the calculated date, partial refunds, and statutory breathing space.

The engine replaced a third-party Java microfinance platform that could not represent the legislated cases correctly. The logic existed only as patches, which do not add up to a design. A year of work, ending in that platform's decommissioning: a layered domain architecture in Python/Django — service layer, DTO boundaries, domain events, and separate calculators for schedule generation, arrears, IRR, EMI and debt crystallisation — behind a test suite larger than the production codebase. The same core served Updraft's loans and the HVAC-financing line HomeServe commissioned and later brought in-house.

I was the main author of its regulatory reporting: Bank of England returns, ESMA securitisation reporting, and the loan-book asset-transfer module.

At the registration end, I stopped a sustained SMS-pumping attack: we were paying the SMS provider more for attack traffic than for genuine customers. Rotating IPs, varied timing and sub-threshold volumes defeated static rules, so I tracked the behavioural signal that separates the two instead — real users complete registration, attackers request codes and never do — and drove AWS WAF rules from it through Cognito triggers and Lambda. The threshold was the hard part: legitimate users abandon registration too. The root cause was architectural: SMS rather than email registration makes those paths attacker-payable by design.
